DECK 01 · BINARY CHOICE: GENERAL THEORY

A switch, modeled as a probability

Vote or not, work or not, mortgage approved or denied. We can't model a 0/1 outcome directly, so every model here is a way of writing P(y = 1 | X).

In plain terms

The question: what makes someone say yes (take a job, get denied a mortgage, vote)? The answer is 0 or 1, but what we really want is the probability of a yes.

The trick: imagine everyone carries a hidden score, how much they lean toward yes. Their characteristics push the score up or down, and some random luck we can't see is added on top. If the score ends up above zero they say yes. We never see the score, only the yes or no.

Probit assumes that random luck follows a normal bell curve. Logit assumes a slightly fatter-tailed bell (the logistic). Either way, each person's characteristics get turned into a probability between 0 and 1 by an S-shaped curve. OLS on the 0/1 outcome (the linear probability model) uses a straight line instead, which is simple but can predict probabilities below 0 or above 1.

Marginal effect = how much the probability of yes changes when one characteristic goes up by one unit. On an S-curve that is biggest for people near 50/50 and tiny for people who are almost sure either way.

Why not just run OLS?

The linear probability model works because the mean of a 0/1 variable is a share. It breaks three ways:

  • Built-in heteroskedasticity. ε takes only two values, so Var = p(1 − p), which moves with X. Fix with robust SEs or WLS.
  • Impossible predictions. X′β escapes [0, 1], and WLS weights go negative.
  • Same effect everywhere. β is identical at p = 0.02 and p = 0.50, but a push matters most for people near the fence.

The fix: a hidden index

Latent model and observation rule\[y_i^* = X_i'\beta + \varepsilon_i,\qquad y_i = \mathbb{1}[y_i^*\ge 0]\]\[P(y=1\mid X) = 1 - F(-X'\beta) \;=\; F(X'\beta)\ \text{ if } F \text{ symmetric}\]

F is the distribution of ε. A CDF is automatically bounded and S-shaped. Example: y* is the gain from working minus staying home.

Estimation by maximum likelihood

Log-likelihood\[\ln L = \sum_{i=1}^n \Big[ y_i \ln F(X_i'\beta) + (1-y_i)\ln\big(1-F(X_i'\beta)\big)\Big]\]

A good β gives high probabilities to people who said yes and low ones to those who said no. No closed form, so the computer climbs the hill. Variance comes from the curvature at the top (the Hessian); then z, Wald, LR or LM tests.

Read the sign, not the size

Only X′β/σ matters: double σ and β and nothing changes. So σ is pinned: 1 for probit, π/√3 ≈ 1.81 for logit. Compare predicted probabilities and marginal effects across models, never raw β.

Marginal effect\[\frac{\partial p_i}{\partial X_{ki}} = f(X_i'\beta)\,\beta_k\]

MEM uses the average person; AME averages everyone's effect.

F24 · Q1

Describe the probit. Why not OLS? What are the marginal effects?

Model answer outline

Write y* = X′β + ε, ε ~ N(0,1), y = 1 if y* ≥ 0, so P(y = 1|X) = Φ(X′β); estimate by ML with ln L = Σ[y ln Φ + (1−y) ln(1−Φ)]. OLS (the LPM) has heteroskedasticity by construction, can predict outside [0,1], and imposes a constant effect. Marginal effects are φ(X′β)βₖ, largest at X′β = 0 (≈ 0.4βₖ), and vary by person; report AME or MEM with delta-method SEs. β is identified only up to scale, so interpret signs and marginal effects. Consequence 1 · the error takes two values\[\varepsilon_i = \begin{cases} 1 - X_i'\beta & \text{w.p. } p_i \\ -X_i'\beta & \text{w.p. } 1-p_i\end{cases}\]\[E[\varepsilon_i\mid X_i]=0 \;\Rightarrow\; p_i = X_i'\beta\]\[\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_i\mid X_i) = p_i(1-X_i'\beta)^2 + (1-p_i)(X_i'\beta)^2 = X_i'\beta\,(1-X_i'\beta)\]

Fixes: OLS + White HCCME, or FWLS: estimate by OLS, compute σ̂ᵢ² = Xᵢ′β̂(1 − Xᵢ′β̂), weight by 1/σ̂ᵢ².

Consequence 2: when Xᵢ′β̂ < 0 or > 1, σ̂ᵢ² < 0. You must set it to an arbitrary small number (0.01? 0.001? 0.0001?), and that choice changes the FWLS estimates.

Consequence 3: E[y|X] = X′β = p, so ∂p/∂Xₖ = βₖ, the same whether p is near 0, 0.5 or 1.

Consequence 4: with X in [XL, XU], the steepest feasible line runs from (XL, 0) to (XU, 1), so a feasible slope needs |β₁| < 1/(XU − XL). P1Show that the LPM error is heteroskedastic, and explain why FWLS can break down.

Given X, y is 1 with probability p = X′β, so ε is 1 − X′β with probability X′β and −X′β with probability 1 − X′β. Then Var(ε|X) = X′β(1 − X′β)² + (1 − X′β)(X′β)² = X′β(1 − X′β), which varies with X. FWLS weights by 1/σ̂ᵢ², but any observation with X′β̂ outside [0, 1] gets a negative variance. Replacing it with an arbitrary small positive number is ad hoc, and the estimates depend on which number you pick.

P2Why can't the LPM just set fitted values below 0 to 0?

The model says y = X′β + ε with E[ε|X] = 0. If X′β < 0, both possible errors (−X′β and 1 − X′β) are positive, so E[ε|X] > 0, which contradicts the model. Truncating the fitted value is a different model from the one estimated. A consistent LPM would need the constraints 0 ≤ Xᵢ′β ≤ 1 imposed in estimation, which also bounds the slope at |β₁| < 1/(XU − XL).

P3A colleague says "logit and probit are pretty much the same." Respond.

True near p = 0.5: once standardized to mean 0 and SD 1, the two CDFs nearly coincide in the middle. False in the tails: logistic tails are thicker, so the models disagree for people with very low or very high predicted probability. More flexible links can differ substantially. The HMDA example shows pregibit spreading effects from about 3 to 25 points where probit forced 7–14. Raw coefficients also aren't comparable because of the scale normalization (σ = 1 vs π/√3); compare predicted probabilities and marginal effects instead.

P4In the random-utility probit, which parameters are identified?

Only β = α₁ − α₀ for variables that enter both utilities, divided by σ = √(σ₁₁ − 2σ₁₀ + σ₀₀). The levels α₀ and α₁ are not separately identified, because only the utility difference drives the choice. σ isn't identified either, since only the sign of y* is observed. A person-level variable that enters both utilities with the same coefficient cancels out.

DECK 02 · BINARY CHOICE: TOPICS

Fit, endogeneity and panels

The randomness lives in the latent index, not in the probability. Every derivation starts from the latent equation, so first, write the model correctly.

In plain terms

Three practical questions about yes/no models. How good is the fit? There is no true R², so we use a likelihood-based index and the share predicted correctly, carefully. What if a regressor is tangled up with the hidden luck? Say income (W) and home ownership are both driven by unseen thrift. The fix: predict W from an instrument, keep the part of W the instrument can't explain, and add it as an extra regressor that "soaks up" the tangled part. What if we watch the same people for several years? Their unobserved traits repeat every year, and we have to account for that.

Spot the wrong probit

The professor flags three common mis-writings. Mark each one.

Measuring fit

McFadden's likelihood ratio index\[LRI = 1 - \frac{\ln L}{\ln L_0}\]

ln L₀ is the intercept-only model. Probabilities are below 1, so ln L < 0 and 0 < LRI < 1. Higher is better, but it is not a share of variance explained. It is tied to the LR test.

Percent correctly predicted. A hit is p̂ ≥ cutoff with y = 1, or p̂ < cutoff with y = 0. With a rare outcome, a 0.5 cutoff predicts "no" for everyone and still scores about 90%. Use the sample share ȳ as the cutoff.

F24

W is endogenous. Does it matter, and how would you fix it?

Model answer outline

Yes: E[ε|W] ≠ 0 makes probit MLE biased and inconsistent. Fix with a control function: OLS first stage of W on Z (Z has an excluded instrument), save η̂, add η̂ to the probit. Under joint normality this is consistent; the t-test on η̂'s coefficient tests exogeneity. Don't plug in Ŵ (2SLS logic fails in nonlinear models). Coefficients are rescaled since Var(ε̃) < 1. Or do full joint MLE.

Lecture study questions, answered

Slides 6–7
SQ 1aShow that ln L is always negative for binary choice models.

Each observation contributes ln P(yᵢ|Xᵢ), where P is a probability strictly between 0 and 1 for any finite X′β. The log of a number in (0, 1) is negative, so the sum is negative. It approaches 0 only if every observation is predicted with probability approaching 1 (perfect separation).

SQ 1bMotivate why 0 < LRI < 1.

The intercept-only model is nested in the full model, so at the maximum ln L₀ ≤ ln L < 0. Dividing by the negative ln L₀ gives 0 < ln L/ln L₀ ≤ 1, so LRI = 1 − ln L/ln L₀ lies in [0, 1). It equals 0 only if the slopes add nothing, and it approaches 1 only as ln L → 0 (perfect prediction), which a finite model never reaches.

SQ 1cIs ln L always negative for a classical linear regression model? Why?

No. The linear model's likelihood is built from densities, not probabilities, and a normal density exceeds 1 whenever σ < 1/√(2π) ≈ 0.40 near its mode. With a small error variance (or y measured in small units) ln L can be positive. So an LRI-type ratio has no guaranteed bounds there, and its value depends on the units of y.

SQ 2Why is the cutoff p̄ = (1/n)Σyᵢ "better" than 0.5 for POPC?

With a rare outcome (p̄ = 0.10, p̂ between 0.01 and 0.40), a 0.5 cutoff predicts y = 0 for everyone. POPC is then 1 − p̄ = 0.90 for any model, even a useless one, and none of the 1s are identified. Using p̄ asks whether the model ranks a person above or below the base rate, so POPC rewards the model for separating the groups. It remains a crude summary that depends on the cutoff.

SQ 3Are LRI and POPC informative when deciding between logit and pregibit? What does "informative" mean?

Not really. "Informative" should mean the statistic leads to a decision with a known error rate. LRI and POPC are descriptive: they have no sampling distribution, ignore the extra parameters pregibit uses, and are dominated by the middle of the distribution. Yet logit and pregibit differ mainly in the tails, where marginal effects differ most. Two models can have nearly identical LRI and POPC and still imply very different effects (HMDA). Use formal tests instead.

SQ 4Discuss Wald, LM and LR tests when choosing among logit, probit and pregibit.

Logit is nested in pregibit at (α, δ) = (0, 0). LR: estimate both, 2(ln Lpreg − ln Llogit) ~ χ²(2). Wald: from the pregibit estimates only, test (α̂, δ̂) = (0, 0). LM: from the logit estimates only, evaluate the pregibit score at α = δ = 0. That is cheapest when pregibit is hard to fit. Probit is only approximately nested (GTL(0.1436, 0) ≈ normal), so testing α = 0.1436, δ = 0 is an approximate test of probit. Logit vs probit is non-nested with the same number of parameters: use a Vuong test.

SQ 5With five slopes, test β₂ = 0.75 and β₄ = β₂ + β₃. How does this relate to LRI, and by how much must LRI differ to reject?

Two restrictions. Estimate the restricted model by substitution: β₂ = 0.75 and β₄ = 0.75 + β₃, so X′β = β₁ + 0.75(X₂ + X₄) + β₃(X₃ + X₄) + β₅X₅ + β₆X₆, with the 0.75(X₂ + X₄) term entering as an offset. Then LR = 2(ln LU − ln LR) ~ χ²(2).

Since ln L = ln L₀(1 − LRI), and ln L₀ is the same intercept-only value for both models: LR = 2(−ln L₀)(LRIU − LRIR). Reject at 5% if LRIU − LRIR > 5.99/(−2 ln L₀). Comparing LRIs is an LR test in disguise, and the needed gap shrinks as n (and thus |ln L₀|) grows.

SQ 6When might you omit the intercept from a binary choice model?

Almost never: the intercept absorbs the base rate and the mean of ε. You might omit it only when theory pins down P(y = 1|X = 0). One example is a random-utility model of two symmetric alternatives with no alternative-specific constant, where X = 0 implies P = F(0) = 0.5. Another is when X already contains a full set of mutually exclusive dummies (the dummy trap). Omitting it otherwise forces the S-curve through F(0) at X = 0 and biases every slope.

Slide 7Couldn't we just divide by qⱼ and say εⱼ ≤ Xⱼ′βⱼ for all observations?

No. qⱼ = −1 for yⱼ = 0, and dividing an inequality by a negative number reverses it: −εⱼ ≤ −Xⱼ′βⱼ means εⱼ ≥ Xⱼ′βⱼ, the opposite region. The q's are what pick the correct tail for each person, and they also flip the sign of the correlation (q₁q₂ρ) when exactly one outcome is 0. Dropping them would assign every observation to the (1,1) quadrant.

Slide 10In Form 1 (γ > 0), what are P(y₁ = 1) and P(y₂ = 1)?

P(y₁ = 1) = Φ(X₁′β₁): y₁'s equation is unchanged. y₂ = 1 can happen with y₁ = 1 (threshold shifted by γ) or with y₁ = 0, so P(y₂ = 1) = Φ₂(X₁′β₁, X₂′β₂ + γ; ρ) + Φ₂(−X₁′β₁, X₂′β₂; −ρ). This is a mixture of two marginal probits, not Φ(X₂′β₂ + γ).

Slide 17Draw the Form 4 boundaries for X₁β₁ = −2, X₂β₂ = 1, γ₁ = 0.25, γ₂ = 0.5. Then repeat with γ₂ = −0.5. Why are the lines perpendicular to the axes in (η₁, η₂) space?

Slide 18 Q1–2What is the economic difference between y₁ entering the y₂ equation (Form 1) and y₁* entering it (Form 3)? Give examples of each.

Form 1: the realized decision matters, a discrete treatment effect. Having health insurance (y₁) changes whether you see a doctor (y₂); being employed changes whether you buy a car. Only the switch matters, not how strongly you wanted it.

Form 3: the underlying propensity matters even when it doesn't cross the threshold. Strength of a mother's career attachment (y₁*) affects her childcare choice even if she is currently not working. A firm's latent profitability of exporting affects its R&D decision. Prefer Form 3 when the intensity behind the choice plausibly drives the other choice; prefer Form 1 when the actual state (insured, employed, married) is what has consequences.

Spouses' labor-supply desires (each partner's willingness to work depends on the other's); two neighboring firms' latent propensities to adopt a technology; a household's desire to own a home and to have children; peer effects between two friends' propensities to smoke. Slide 19 / 31Why is there no "bivariate logit"? Why is there one after all?

There isn't: unlike the normal, there is no standard bivariate logistic distribution with logistic marginals and a free correlation parameter spanning (−1, 1). Gumbel's bivariate logistic has fixed, limited dependence. So no natural analogue of Φ₂ exists.

There is: with a copula. Take logistic marginals Fⱼ(εⱼ) = 1/(1 + e^(−εⱼ)) and glue them with a Gaussian copula C = Φ₂(Φ⁻¹(F₁), Φ⁻¹(F₂); θ). That is a valid bivariate model with logit marginals; the calculator above does exactly this.

Slide 19 Q2One regressor in the first equation is correlated with ε₁. Is the bivariate probit estimator still appropriate? What is a feasible method?

No: with E[ε₁|W] ≠ 0, the likelihood is misspecified and β₁, ρ (and through ρ, β₂) are inconsistent. Feasible methods: (1) a control function: OLS first stage of W on X₁ and an excluded instrument Z, then add the residual η̂ to the first equation of the bivariate probit (consistent under joint normality, with rescaled coefficients and bootstrapped SEs); or (2) full MLE of the three-equation system: a trivariate normal for (ε₁, ε₂, η), written as f(η) × the bivariate probit probability conditional on η.

Slide 23What makes a function F₁(ε₁) a valid CDF?

Non-decreasing; right-continuous; lim F = 0 as ε → −∞ and lim F = 1 as ε → +∞. Any such F can be a copula marginal, and the two marginals need not come from the same family.

Slide 27What happens to the Clayton copula as θ → 0?

(t^(−θ) − 1)/θ → −ln t, so φ⁻¹(φ(F₁) + φ(F₂)) = exp(ln F₁ + ln F₂) = F₁F₂: the product copula, independence. Kendall's τ = θ/(θ + 2) → 0. As θ → ∞, Clayton approaches perfect positive dependence.

Estimate both by MLE. For each person compute ℓ₁ᵢ = ln P̂probit(yᵢ|Xᵢ) and ℓ₂ᵢ = ln P̂logit(yᵢ|Xᵢ), using the estimated cutpoints. SQ 7Debate: "The estimated cutpoints of ordered probit should equal those of ordered logit; if not, that's misspecification."

Picking one option from several

Travel mode, vacation spot, phone carrier, job status. Uⱼ = Vⱼ + εⱼ, and you pick the highest. The modeling choices are what goes into V and how ε is distributed.

In plain terms

Picking one option from several unranked ones: subway, bus, car or bike. Each option gives you a "utility" (how good it is for you) plus some random taste. You pick whichever is best. Under the multinomial logit the probabilities are each option's e^V divided by the sum over all options. Its famous side effect is IIA: the odds of subway vs car don't care what other options exist. That is wrong when two options are near-copies, like a blue bus and a red bus.

Choice probability from random utility

CL and MNL are one family

"Socioeconomic variables drop out" is how people often describe CL, but that is too limiting: interact them with alternative dummies.

Gumbel facts and identification

\[F(\varepsilon) = e^{-e^{-\varepsilon}},\quad E = \gamma\approx 0.5772,\quad \operatorname{Var} = \pi^2/6\approx 1.6449\]\[P(y=j) = \frac{e^{V_j}}{\sum_k e^{V_k}} = \frac{1}{1 + \sum_{k\ne j} e^{V_k - V_j}}\]
Derivation: Train (2009), §3.10.
  • CL: the intercept vanishes; only slopes are identified.
  • MNL: adding a constant vector to every αⱼ leaves αₖ − αⱼ unchanged, so set α₁ = 0; you estimate α₂ − α₁, …, αJ − α₁.
  • Scale: all εⱼ share the Gumbel variance π²/6; rescaling it rescales every parameter in proportion. Independence and homoskedasticity are key assumptions.

Weakness 1: no random tastes

Weakness 2: proportional substitution (Train)

\[\frac{\%\Delta P_{ki}}{\%\Delta A_{mj}} = -\beta_m A_{mj} P_{ji}\qquad \text{for every } k\ne j\]

The right side has nothing about k: a 1% rise in the price of j cuts every other option's probability by the same percent (e.g., −2.5% each). A closer substitute l should lose more than k. The simulator above shows this when the blue bus improves.

Hausman–McFadden test, precisely

θ = (α₂, …, αJ−1). θ̂(J) uses all alternatives; θ̂(J−1) drops alternative J and the people who chose it. Under H₀ (IIA) both are consistent and θ̂(J) is efficient. If J causes the violation, only θ̂(J−1) is consistent. Three problems (Vijverberg, IZA 2011): H₁ must be specified precisely, because if another alternative causes the violation both estimators are inconsistent; the asymptotically χ² statistic regularly comes out negative, meaning either the test is broken or it is computed incorrectly; and the room for choosing what to drop invites deceptive reporting.

Nested multinomial logit (NMNL)

Sort-of-multivariate Gumbel: branches B₁…B_K, each with twigs\[F(\varepsilon_i) = \exp\!\Big(-\sum_{k=1}^K\Big[\sum_{j\in B_k} e^{-\varepsilon_{ji}/\lambda_k}\Big]^{\lambda_k}\Big)\qquad \lambda_k = 1\ \forall k \Rightarrow \text{MNL}\]\[P(j\in B_k) = \frac{e^{V_j/\lambda_k}\Big[\sum_{m\in B_k} e^{V_m/\lambda_k}\Big]^{\lambda_k-1}}{\sum_{l=1}^K\Big[\sum_{m\in B_l}e^{V_m/\lambda_l}\Big]^{\lambda_l}}\]\[V_{ji} = W_{ki} + Y_{ji}:\quad P(j\mid B_k) = \frac{e^{Y_j/\lambda_k}}{\sum_{m\in B_k}e^{Y_m/\lambda_k}},\quad P(B_k) = \frac{e^{W_k + \lambda_k I_k}}{\sum_l e^{W_l+\lambda_l I_l}},\quad I_k = \ln\sum_{m\in B_k}e^{Y_m/\lambda_k}\]
  • Correlation within a nest is inversely related to λₖ: λ = 1 none, 0 < λ < 1 positive.
  • McFadden: 0 ≤ λₖ ≤ 1 is consistent with utility maximization for all values of the variables; λₖ < 0 never is; λₖ > 1 only for some values.
  • Pⱼ/Pₘ depends on more than Vⱼ − Vₘ when j and m sit on different branches, so IIA no longer holds across nests.
  • Two-stage estimation (MNL within each branch, then MNL over branches) is possible; FIML in one step is efficient.
  • Extensions (Train ch. 5): 3-level nests, paired combinatorial logit, generalized nested logit. NMNL can't handle panel correlation over time; random-effects logit, MNL and NMNL are mixed-logit special cases.

Multinomial probit (MNP)

SQ 1Are CL and MNL the same model or different models?

The same model family. Both come from independent Gumbel errors and give e^(Vⱼ)/Σe^(Vₖ). They differ only in what enters V: alternative attributes with common coefficients (CL) vs person characteristics with alternative-specific coefficients (MNL). MNL can be written as a CL with alternative dummies interacted with person traits, and a mixed specification uses both.

SQ 2What is the main strength of NMNL? The main weakness?

Strength: it relaxes IIA across nests (close substitutes compete more) while keeping closed-form probabilities, and it is consistent with utility maximization for 0 ≤ λ ≤ 1. Weakness: the tree must be specified in advance, results can be sensitive to it, and IIA still holds within each nest and between branches.

SQ 3aIn what aspects does NMNL exhibit IIA?

Within a branch, P(j|Bₖ) is an MNL, so the odds between two twigs in the same nest don't depend on anything else. At the upper level, P(Bₖ) is an MNL over branches, so the odds between two branches don't depend on other branches (given the inclusive values). IIA fails only for pairs of twigs in different nests.

SQ 3bHow might you test whether the chosen nesting is appropriate?

Test λₖ = 1 for each nest (Wald, or LR of NMNL vs MNL). If you can't reject, the nest adds nothing. Estimates outside (0, 1] signal a wrong tree. Run Hausman–McFadden tests within nests (where IIA should hold). Compare rival trees: they are non-nested, so use Vuong or information criteria.

SQ 4NYC {Walk, Bus, Train, Taxi, Bike, Car, Uber, Lyft, scooter}: explain IIA; does CL have it; how to check; how to nest; how to validate the nesting?

IIA: the odds of Uber vs Train depend only on their own utilities. A new or improved option draws share from all others in proportion, so a cheaper Lyft would pull as much (proportionally) from walking as from Uber, which is implausible. CL has the same IIA property: same Gumbel structure, only V differs. To check: a Hausman–McFadden test dropping Lyft (Uber's close substitute) and seeing whether the other coefficients move; better, estimate NMNL and test λ = 1. Nesting: {for-hire: Taxi, Uber, Lyft}, {transit: Bus, Train}, {active/micro: Walk, Bike, scooter}, {Car}. Validate: λ̂ in (0, 1) and significantly below 1, LR vs MNL, and Vuong against alternative trees (e.g., putting scooter with for-hire).

SQ 5MNL, three alternatives, four socioeconomic variables; the first has a random coefficient. Write it, count identified parameters, describe estimation, and test randomness.
\[V_{ji} = S_{1i}\alpha_{1ji} + \sum_{k=2}^4 S_{ki}\alpha_{kj},\qquad \alpha_{1ji} = \bar\alpha_{1j} + \sigma_j u_{ji},\quad u_{ji}\sim N(0,1)\]

Interpretation: S₁'s effect on the utility of j varies across people with mean ᾱ₁ⱼ and spread σⱼ, so the model reports the share of people for whom S₁ raises j relative to the base. The 3 other variables give 2 × 3 = 6 fixed coefficients. Estimation: simulated ML. The null is on the boundary (σ ≥ 0), so the LR statistic follows a 50:50 mixture of χ²(0) and χ²(1); halve the usual p-value.

Two ways data get cut off

Truncation: cases below the limit are missing entirely, no y and no X. Survey only Hawaii vacationers; non-travelers never appear.

Five final notes on tobit

  1. One process decides both zero vs positive and how much. Alternative: Cragg's (1971) two-part model, a probit then a regression; a special case of sample selection (see Amemiya's "Types" of tobit).
  2. MLE is inconsistent if ε isn't normal. QMLE helps only if the true distribution is in the linear exponential family.
  3. Heteroskedasticity σᵢ² = σ² exp(Zᵢ′α): P(yᵢ* < 0) = Φ(−Xᵢ′β/(σ exp(Zᵢ′α/2))). Ignoring it when X and Z are correlated makes β absorb both effects, so β̂ is inconsistent; MLE of (β, σ², α) fixes it.
  4. More flexible distributions: GTL, skewed-t, generalized extreme value. If they nest normality, normality can be tested.
  5. Semiparametric: CLAD, SCLS (Cameron & Trivedi §16.9).

DECK 07 · SAMPLE SELECTION

Who's in the data depends on the outcome

Wages are seen only for people who work. The target is E[wage offer] for everyone, not E[wage | working]. Always ask: could the choices that put people in my sample relate to the outcome?

In plain terms

We see wages only for people who work, and workers aren't a random slice of everyone: motivated people both work more and earn more. Averaging workers' wages overstates what non-workers would be offered. Heckman's fix: model who works (a probit), then add a correction term, the inverse Mills ratio λ, to the wage regression. λ measures how "unusually lucky" the included people are. It works best if some variable affects working but not wages (the exclusion restriction, or "instrument").

In plain terms

Sometimes data get cut off at a limit. Truncation: people below the limit are missing entirely, like surveying only people who vacationed in Hawaii. Censoring: they're in the data, but their value is stuck at the limit, like hours worked recorded as 0 for everyone not working, or a scale that can't read below zero. Either way, a plain regression line comes out too flat. Maximum-likelihood models (truncated regression, tobit) use the known shape of the cut-off to undo the damage.

Heckman / Heckit / Type-2 Tobit\[\text{Participation: } y_1^* = X_1'\beta_1 + \varepsilon_1,\quad y_1 = \mathbb{1}[y_1^*\ge 0]\]\[\text{Outcome: } y_2^* = X_2'\beta_2 + \varepsilon_2,\quad \text{seen only if } y_1 = 1,\qquad \operatorname{Corr}(\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_2)=\rho\]\[E[y_2\mid y_1=1] = X_2'\beta_2 + \rho\sigma_2\,\lambda(X_1'\beta_1),\qquad \lambda = \frac{\phi}{\Phi}\]

ρ is the whole story. If ρ = 0, P(y₁ = 1, y₂) = P(y₁ = 1)·f(y₂): OLS on workers is fine. Selection on observables alone does no harm; correlated unobservables bias things. Motivated people both work more and earn more.

Two-step: (1) probit on everyone → λ̂ᵢ; (2) OLS of y₂ on X₂ and λ̂ᵢ for workers. t-test on λ's coefficient tests for selection. Always report the participation equation.

Slide 18Does it matter from which part of the model the "observable factors" are omitted: participation, outcome, or both?

Both equations: the omitted factor sits in ε₁ and ε₂, which creates ρ ≠ 0 and selection bias, plus ordinary omitted-variable bias. Outcome only: it lands in ε₂; this is classic OVB if correlated with X₂, and it induces ρ ≠ 0 if it is also correlated with the selection index (through X₁ or ε₁). Participation only: it lands in ε₁; it creates ρ ≠ 0 only if it is related to the outcome's unobservables. If it is a pure selection shifter (an exclusion restriction), omitting it doesn't bias β₂ through ρ, but it throws away the instrument, so identification falls back on λ's nonlinearity and precision collapses. This is why you always report the participation equation.

Slide 25Why are ε̃₂ᵢ and β̂₁ − β₁ independent? Show how Ω gives the correct variance.

β̂₁ comes from the probit on the full sample, so asymptotically β̂₁ − β₁ is a linear combination of the probit scores, which are functions of y₁ and X₁ only. By construction ε̃₂ᵢ = ε₂ᵢ − E[ε₂ᵢ|y₁ᵢ = 1, X] has mean zero conditional on the selection outcome and the regressors, so it is uncorrelated with any function of (y₁, X₁). Hence the cross term vanishes asymptotically, and the two pieces of Ω simply add. With W = [X₂, λ̂] for the selected sample:

\[\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta_2,\hat\beta_\lambda) = (W'W)^{-1}W'\Omega W(W'W)^{-1},\qquad \Omega = \operatorname{diag}\big(\sigma_2^2(1-\rho^2\delta_i)\big) + \beta_\lambda^2\,\Lambda\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta_1)\Lambda'\]

The second term is a full n×n matrix, which is exactly what HCCME misses.

Slide 49Why is it necessary (not optional) for Robinson's approach that X₁ contain a variable excluded from X₂?

Robinson's method lets g(X₁′β₁) be any function. If X₁ = X₂, then g(X₂′β₁) can absorb any function of X₂, including the linear part X₂′β₂, so β₂ is not identified. The normal-λ version escapes only through its specific functional form, which Robinson deliberately gives up. An excluded variable makes X₁′β₁ move independently of X₂, so the partialling-out step leaves variation in X₂ to identify β₂.

Slide 55MNL selection: if someone is very likely to be wage-employed, what are pᵢ, zᵢ, λᵢ? Does βλ = ρσ₂ still hold?

pᵢ → 1, so zᵢ = Φ⁻¹(pᵢ) → +∞ and λᵢ = φ(zᵢ)/pᵢ → 0: almost no correction, because a near-certain participant is not selected on unobservables. βλ = ρσ₂ still holds, but ρ is now the correlation between ε₂ and the transformed selection error (Lee's approach maps the MNL index into a standard normal and assumes that transformed error is jointly normal with ε₂). It measures selection on unobservables into wage work specifically; its sign tells whether wage workers earn more (+) or less (−) than a random person with the same X₂.

Slide 60In the Roy examples, what besides sector earnings enters the utility comparison? Give three other applications.

Union: job security, grievance procedures, benefits, ideology, union dues. Public sector: job security, pensions, mission or public service motivation, hours flexibility. Movers: moving costs, family ties, amenities, climate, housing costs. Slide 64 Q1How does the extended Roy model differ from yᵢ = Xᵢ′β + γDᵢ + εᵢ (D = union)?

The dummy regression (a) imposes the same returns to X in both sectors, with only an intercept shift γ; (b) treats D as exogenous, ignoring that workers choose a sector on unobserved earnings advantages, so E[ε|D] ≠ 0 and γ̂ is biased; (c) assumes one error variance. The Roy model has sector-specific β₂, β₃, σ₂, σ₃, a selection equation, and λ terms correcting each sector's mean.

Slide 64 Q2How does it differ from yᵢ = DᵢXᵢ′β₁ + (1 − Dᵢ)Xᵢ′β₂ + εᵢ with sector-specific variances?

This switching regression fixes (a) and (c) by allowing different returns and variances, but still treats D as exogenous. There are no λ terms, so if ρ₁₂ ≠ 0 or ρ₁₃ ≠ 0 each sector's coefficients are biased by selection, exactly the Heckman problem applied to each sector. Roy adds the choice equation and the correction terms +ρ₁₂σ₂φ/Φ and −ρ₁₃σ₃φ/(1 − Φ).

Slide 65The suggested Monte Carlo exercises: what would you expect?

Converting ε₁ to a standardized t(5) or χ²(5) while estimating with normal-based Heckit misspecifies λ. The bias is most severe without an exclusion restriction, since then identification rests entirely on λ's normal-specific shape. Making ε₂ non-normal hurts MLE (wrong likelihood) more than the two-step, whose second stage needs only the conditional mean of ε₂ to be linear in ε₁.

DECK 08 · COUNT MODELS, PART 1

Outcomes that count: 0, 1, 2, …

Doctor visits, patents, derogatory credit remarks, trips. Non-negative integers, skewed, often many zeros, and the variance grows with the mean. OLS ignores all of that.

In plain terms

For outcomes that count things: 0, 1, 2 doctor visits. The Poisson model assumes events happen randomly at a steady rate, which forces the average and the spread (variance) to be equal. Real counts are usually more spread out than that (overdispersion). The negative binomial lets each person have their own random rate, which adds extra spread. A nice fact: Poisson regression still gets the coefficients right if the average is modeled correctly; only the standard errors need fixing.

Poisson\[P(y) = \frac{\lambda^y e^{-\lambda}}{y!},\qquad E[y] = \operatorname{Var}(y) = \lambda,\qquad \lambda_i = e^{X_i'\beta}\]\[\ln L = \sum_i\big[y_iX_i'\beta - e^{X_i'\beta} - \ln y_i!\big],\qquad \text{FOC: } \sum_i(y_i - \hat\lambda_i)X_i = 0\]

Equidispersion comes from many tiny independent time slices. βₖ ≈ % change in the expected count per unit Xₖ; marginal effect = λᵢβₖ.

Slide 18 Q1How would Wald, LR and Vuong tests help distinguish NegBin 1, NegBin 2 and NegBin P?

Slide 18 Q2Statistically, how would you choose among Poisson, NegBin and Normal–Poisson count models?

Poisson is nested in NegBin (α = 0) and in Normal–Poisson (σ = 0). The Cameron–Trivedi test is a quick check. NegBin vs Normal–Poisson are non-nested (gamma vs lognormal mixing), so use Vuong, possibly with an AIC/BIC adjustment if parameter counts differ. Too many zeros, endogeneity, panels

Greene's credit data (n = 13,444): 81% of people have zero derogatory remarks, but a plain Poisson with λ̂ = 0.46 predicts only 63%. Poisson ties zeros to the mean, so it can't fit both.

In plain terms

Real count data often have too many zeros: 81% of credit applicants have zero bad remarks. Maybe there are two kinds of zero: people who would never have a remark, and people who could but happened not to. The zero-inflated Poisson mixes a "never" group with a regular Poisson group. Fix 1: truncated Poisson (positives only)\[g(y) = \frac{p(y)}{1-p(0)},\ \ y = 1,2,\dots\qquad E[y\mid y\gt 0] = \frac{\lambda}{1-e^{-\lambda}}\]

How long until something happens

Unemployment spells, job tenure, time to default. Spells still running at the interview are censored: we only know they lasted at least this long. t is time in the spell, not calendar time.

In plain terms

"How long until it happens?": time unemployed, time until default. The key idea is the hazard: among people still waiting right now, the chance of leaving in the next moment. Some spells are still running when we collect the data (censored), and we know only that they lasted at least that long. The Cox model lets the hazard over time take any shape (say, a spike when unemployment benefits run out) and only asks how characteristics scale it up or down.

The hazard\[\lambda(t) = \frac{f(t)}{S(t)},\qquad S(t) = 1-F(t)\]\[\text{(1) choose } \lambda(t)\quad\text{(2) } \Lambda(t) = \int_0^t\lambda(s)\,ds\quad \text{(3) } S(t) = e^{-\Lambda(t)},\ f(t) = \lambda(t)S(t)\]

λ(t)dt is the chance of leaving in the next instant, given you lasted until t. Duration dependence: positive if λ rises with t, negative if it falls. Constant λ = exponential.

Likelihood with censored spells\[\ln L = \sum_i\big[d_i\ln\lambda(t_i) + \ln S(t_i)\big]\]

dᵢ = 1 if completed. Completed spells contribute f(t); censored ones S(t). Same logic as tobit.

Slide 33 Q1Aₖ has several exiters and Xm rises by the same amount for each i ∈ Aₖ. What happens to P(Aₖ)?

Write P = SA/SR with SA = Σᵢ∈Aₖ e^(Xᵢ′β), which is part of SR. A common shift raises each term in SA by the factor e^(βmΔ) in both numerator and denominator, giving dP/dΔ = βmP(1 − P), the same form as for one person.

Slide 33 Q2–3Xm rises equally for everyone in the risk set (e.g., UI benefits raised for all). What happens to P(Aₖ)?

Nothing. Every term in numerator and denominator gets multiplied by e^(βmΔ), which cancels, whether Aₖ has one member or several. The partial likelihood identifies β only from differences across people in the risk set. A change common to everyone shifts the overall exit rate, but that is absorbed into the unspecified baseline λ₀(t), so its effect isn't identified from that interval's comparison.

DECK 11 · REPLICATION, PART 1

Publication bias

If significant results are more likely to be published, the published record overstates effects even when every individual study is honest.

In plain terms

If journals prefer statistically significant results, the published record is a filtered, biased sample, even when every author is honest. Small or zero effects get published only when luck makes them look big. These lectures model that filter, show how often a "finding" is really true (PPV), and show how to detect bias from the pattern of published t-statistics.

Andrews–Kasy\[\beta^*\sim N(\beta,\sigma^2),\qquad z = \beta^*/\sigma,\qquad p(z) = \begin{cases}1 & |z|\ge 1.96\\ q & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}\]\[f_{pub}(\beta^*) = \frac{f(\beta^*)\,p(z)}{P(\text{publish})}\]

Bias is largest for moderate true effects. Tiny effects get filtered roughly symmetrically; huge effects pass anyway.

Uncovering publication bias

  • Definition: "certain types of statistical results are more likely to be published than other results, conditional on the research design and the data used."
  • Ideally delineate the universe of β* (SSRN, RePEc, anecdotes): almost surely incomplete. Note significant but economically trivial effects (an elasticity of 0.02?).
  • Caliper: compare 1.96 < t < 2.01 with 1.91 < t < 1.96. Without manipulation the ratio is slightly below 1, since the density falls: [Φ(2.01) − Φ(1.96)]/[Φ(1.96) − Φ(1.91)] = 0.907 (0.822 for ±0.10 windows). In economics it is well above 1.
  • Under no relationship, p-values should be uniform; published ones show two modes and almost nothing near 1.
  • If β > 0, t should grow with n. In economics it doesn't.
  • Hedges (1992) meta-analysis can estimate the share of unpublished estimates.
  • Brodeur & Lé (AEJ Applied 2016): spikes at 2.00 or 1.50 come from too few reported digits; a raw "0.02" p-value maps to z between 2.24 and 2.43.

Gechert & Heimberger (EER 2022): 441 estimates of ∂g/∂T from 42 studies, mean −0.019, meta-regression SCᵢⱼ = β₀ + β₁SEᵢⱼ + uᵢⱼ. Lecture study questions, answered

Slide 19
SQ 1A field studies 200 distinct relationships. What do R = 25, 4, 1, 0.5 mean?

R = true : false relationships, so true = 200R/(R + 1). R = 25: about 192 true, 8 false: almost everything studied is real. R = 4: 160 true, 40 false. R = 1: 100 and 100. R = 0.5: about 67 true and 133 false, so most hypotheses tested are wrong, and false positives will make up a large share of claimed findings.

SQ 2Without testing, why is PPV = R/(R + 1)?

With no test, a "claimed" relationship is just a randomly picked one, so the chance it is true is the share of true relationships in the pool: R true for every 1 false gives R/(R + 1). Mathematically, set the test to accept everything (1 − β = 1, α = 1) and PPV = R/(R + 1).

SQ 3Does PPV rise or fall when testing takes place? Conceptually and mathematically.

Conceptually: a test filters, keeping true relationships at rate 1 − β (power) and false ones at rate α. It raises the share of truths among survivors if it keeps truths more often than falsehoods. Mathematically: PPVtest/PPVpre = (1 − β)(R + 1)/[(1 − β)R + α] > 1 ⇔ (1 − β)(R + 1) > (1 − β)R + α ⇔ 1 − β > α. Any test with power above its size raises PPV. A test with power below its size (a perverse or badly misspecified test) would lower it.

SQ 4Does PPV rise or fall when researchers cheat (U > 0)? Why?

Conceptually: cheating converts a share U of all failed tests into "findings." Most failed tests concern false relationships (fraction 1 − α of the false ones fail, versus β of the true ones), so the added findings are disproportionately false. Mathematically: PPV(U) = [(1−β)R + UβR]/[(1−β)R + α + U(βR + 1 − α)]. It is a weighted mix of the honest PPV and the "PPV of manipulated results," βR/(βR + 1 − α). It falls whenever βR/(βR + 1 − α) < (1−β)R/((1−β)R + α), which simplifies to β/(1 − α) < (1 − β)/α, i.e. αβ < (1 − α)(1 − β), true for any sensible test (α < 1 − β). Example: R = 1, power 0.8, α = 0.05: 0.94 → 0.85 at U = 0.1 → 0.72 at U = 0.3.

DECK 12 · REPLICATION, PART 2

Specification searches and research ethics

Torture the data and they will confess. Leamer (1983): death-penalty effects on murder ranged from −12 to +27 depending on specification.

In plain terms

Try enough specifications and one will be "significant" by luck: torture the data and they confess. These lectures cover what replication means (verify, reanalyze, reproduce), real fraud cases, and the proposed fixes: adjust p-values for multiple tests, average over all models (BMA), show every reasonable specification (the specification curve), and lock in the analysis plan in advance (pre-registration).

Three meanings of replication

  • Verification: same data, same code. Stronger: rebuild the sample from raw source.
  • Reanalysis: same data, other reasonable choices. Did the authors' choices slant it?
  • Reproduction: new data (another wave, group, country). Check definitions match.

Dutch survey of 6,800 researchers: 51% used at least one questionable practice in 3 years; 8% fabricated or falsified. Stapel: 58 retractions. Also Gino, Tessier-Lavigne, paper mills at Wiley. Pressures: grants, positions, demand for clean results.

Replication logistics

Verification can use the author's data and do-file, or more thoroughly access the original source, rebuild the sample and write your own code. Reanalysis examines sample construction and analysis choices (variable definitions and transformations, specification, estimator, test statistics), possibly extending them; see Borjas–Breznau. Reproduction uses a different gender or race group, the next CPS wave, a different survey or another country: check the questionnaire, answer codes and skip patterns. Does a variable with the same name measure the same thing?

Access problems: proprietary data (Compustat, IRS, firm data) can't be posted; public micro data aren't posted at journal sites; do-files that build samples, deflators and other macro inputs go missing; redefinitions and seasonal re-adjustment overwrite historical series. JAE requires data; others vary, and editors don't always enforce their policies.

Chang & Li (FRB WP 2015-083; Crit. Fin. Rev. 2021): 67 studies in 13 journals (July 2008 – Oct 2013): 6 proprietary; of 35 in journals with a sharing requirement, 28 available; of 26 without, 11 available (on request). 39/61 = 64% provided data; 29/39 = 76% replicated (≈ 48% of 61).

Details on the fixes

  • Data sharing (Brodeur et al., IZA DP 15586, 2022): 38,876 test statistics, 1,106 articles, 25 journals (13 with policies), 2002–2020, from DID, IV, RCT and RDD papers only. No effect of sharing policy or data type (administrative, third-party survey, own-collected, other) on p-hacking.
  • RCTs: a more rigid method (Brodeur et al. 2016), but see Andrews–Kasy on Camerer's experiments. p-hacking = reporting the most significant specification; instrument hacking = reporting the most favorable first- or second-stage statistics (Keane, Neal & Vu, NBER WP 35633, 2026).
  • BMA: p(m|D) = p(D|m)p(m)/p(D), where 1/p(D) is the constant making Σ p(m|D) = 1; δ* = Σₘ p(m|D)δ̂ₘ. Fernández, Ley & Steel (JAE 2001): 140 countries, 41 regressors (72 countries complete), 2⁴¹ ≈ 2.2 × 10¹² OLS models, following Sala-i-Martin's "I just ran two (four) million regressions." Other BMA uses: housing WTP, soccer player values (with endogeneity), education assessment, corporate default, Chilean dropout (discrete-time competing risks).
  • General-to-specific (Hendry, LSE): start general and prune. The general model still embeds choices (Y or ln Y? LPM, probit or logit? outliers?). ML "experts": you still have to specify the experts. Nonparametric: weaker assumptions but data-hungry.
  • Specification curve (SSN 2015/2019/2020): hurricane example (Jung et al., PNAS 2014), 1,728 specifications; p-value of a positive effect = 0.85. The bootstrap works because name femininity is as-good-as-random. Non-experimental idea: filter ỹₘ = y − β̂ₘX with model m, then check that every other model l finds δ̂ₗ ≈ 0 (akin to the Davidson–MacKinnon J test).
  • Many outcomes: instead of reporting only significant βₘ, stack the M equations SUR-style and Wald-test H₀: β₁ = … = βM = 0.
  • Adjusted p-values (Harvey, Lin & Zhu, RFS 2016): Bonferroni p = min(Mpᵢ, 1); Holm; Benjamini–Hochberg–Yekutieli. Finance anomalies need t ≈ 3.5.
  • White's Reality Check (Ecta 2000): split the sample, estimate many models on part 1, forecast part 2, bootstrap, and count how often the preferred model really wins. Similar to cross-validation.
  • AEA RCT Registry (est. April 2012; 5,083 studies in 160 countries by Oct 2021). Brodeur et al. (IZA DP 15476, 2022): 15,992 test statistics, 314 articles, 15 journals, 2018–2021. Pre-registration is more common later, with more coauthors, and less common with a higher share of women authors; no difference by experience or affiliation rank. Registered vs not: same bump at |z| = 2 (KS p > 0.10, caliper p = 0.51). With a PAP: smaller bump and smaller caliper gaps.

Questions the slides pose, answered

Slides 5, 44
Slide 5Why is the p-value the same for (m, k) = (10, 1) and (50, 5)?

Lovell's formula 1 − (1 − α)^(m/k) depends only on the ratio m/k = 10, a rough rule of thumb. The exact calculation treats the number of false positives K among m independent tests as binomial(m, α). Finding at least 1 in 10 tries (P = 0.401) is far more likely than finding at least 5 in 50 tries (P ≈ 0.104), because the binomial concentrates around mα = 2.5 when m = 50. So the rule overstates the second case. The broader lesson stands either way: repeated searching inflates the true p-value well above the nominal 0.05.

Slide 44Does a pre-analysis plan prevent open-minded search, stifle innovation, or disadvantage young researchers? Is mining old RCT data to design a new PAP legitimate?

For PAPs: they remove researcher degrees of freedom, and the evidence shows smaller bumps at |z| = 2 when a detailed PAP exists. Against: research is a search for knowledge; a rigid plan may block learning from the data, favor well-funded teams who can afford RCTs, and slow junior researchers who need publications quickly. Balance: report pre-specified analyses as confirmatory and label everything else exploratory. Mining existing data to form a hypothesis, then testing it on new data under a PAP, is legitimate: it is the hold-out logic of White's Reality Check. It is wasteful only if the confirmatory study is never run, and unethical only if the exploration is hidden. Registries for observational studies are harder because the data already exist when the plan is written; time-stamped plans before data access, or hold-out samples, are partial fixes.
